Leicester Care Assistant Reveals How She Rebuilt Her Career Identity in 21 Days, Even After Moving Abroad Made Her Feel Like Nobody

Thursday, 30 October 2026 · by Adaeze Okonkwo, Leicester, UK

It’s 2am and you’re awake again.

The house is quiet. Your shift finished hours ago, your body still runs on night-shift time, and everyone who could talk to you is asleep. So you do the thing you’d never do in daylight. You pick up your phone and search: “did I make a mistake moving abroad?”

Back home, you had a title. A team. A desk with your name on it. People stood up when you walked into the room, and your opinion actually moved things.

Now you wipe strangers. Or scan parcels. Or stack shelves at hours your former colleagues back in Lagos, Abuja or Port Harcourt would not believe. Nobody here knows you ever managed anything.

Online, you post the good parts. The snow. The new phone. The Sunday rice that actually tasted like home. The timeline looks fine.

But your mother tells her friends you’re “doing well abroad,” and you haven’t corrected her. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

You’ve applied to dozens of jobs. You’ve rewritten your CV more times than you can count. A handful of replies, a couple of interviews, nothing that sees who you actually are.

You’ve tried the WhatsApp groups. Plenty of sympathy, plenty of gossip, no path. You’ve watched the motivational videos — the feeling lasts until morning, then the alarm goes off and it’s the same shift again.

And the heaviest part is not the work. It’s the quiet belief that you’re the only one this happened to. That everyone else Japa’d and landed softly, and you alone fell.

You’re not the only one. And it’s not because you’re lazy, or because you made one wrong decision. Something specific happened to your identity when you moved — and something specific can rebuild it.

Here’s what changed it for me.

In the next few minutes, I’m going to show you the exact 21-day plan that pulled me out of that 2am spiral — about 30 minutes a day — and how you can run the same system for yourself.

My story — the honest version

Four years ago in Lagos, I was a banking officer. I had a team of six reporting to me, a corner desk, and customers who asked for me by name. Mrs Okonkwo. I knew my numbers, I knew my people, and I knew exactly who I was.

Then we relocated to the UK — my husband’s job moved us, and honestly, we wanted our daughter to grow up with options we never had. I told everyone it was the right decision. I believed it too.

What I didn’t plan for was this: in Leicester, with a Nigerian banking CV that recruiters here treated like a foreign document, the only role that came back to me was care assistant, night shifts.

I took it. Rent doesn’t negotiate.

The first month, I told myself it was temporary. The second month, I stopped talking at dinner. By the third, I had mastered the art of the reassuring Sunday message home — photos of me smiling, carefully cropped so you couldn’t see the staff room behind me. My mother told everyone her daughter was “doing well abroad.” I never corrected her.

The real cost wasn’t the sore feet. It was smaller than that and heavier. It was going silent with my own family. It was declining calls from Lagos colleagues because I couldn’t bear the question “so how is work over there?” It was standing in a resident’s room at 3am, doing work that matters — I want to be clear, care work matters — while feeling like the person who once signed off six people’s work had been deleted.

So I did what a responsible adult does. I fixed my CV and applied again.

Rewrite number one used my real Lagos titles. Nothing. Rewrite number four added UK keywords I copied from advice videos. Two replies, both rejecting me. By rewrite number ten, I had stripped so much of my actual experience that the CV described a stranger — and it still didn’t work. I was applying through every board and recruiter I could find, dozens of applications, and the silence was answering a question I didn’t want to hear: maybe my Nigerian experience counts for nothing here.

I tried the Naija WhatsApp groups next. You know exactly how those go — real warmth, real prayer, and absolutely no plan. Sympathy in the morning, gossip by evening, and nobody who had actually climbed out.

Then motivation. YouTube at 2am, Instagram reels on the bus. “Your time is coming.” It always wore off by the next shift. You can’t motivate your way out of a structural problem.

And then the part I almost don’t want to write: I paid for a course. £20 — about £35,000 in the money I used to earn. A generic “UK career success” course. It told me to network confidently and believe in myself. It never once mentioned what it’s like when your five years of management happened in Lagos and the recruiter reading your CV has never heard of your bank. It wasn’t written for me. I finished it feeling the same, minus twenty pounds.

So I did the most dangerous thing of all: nothing. I stayed quiet and told myself “it’s only for now.” For months, that sentence did the job of a plan.

The turning point was a 2am search. After a night shift, sitting on the edge of my bed so I wouldn’t wake anyone, I finally typed the question properly — not “how to get a UK job” this time, but something closer to the truth: “why do I feel like nobody after moving abroad?”

What I found wasn’t motivation. It was clinical language for what had happened to me. Researchers have names for it: identity disruption, status loss, acculturative stress. Reading those pages was the first time in two years something had described my situation exactly — not the generic “expat” story, the Nigerian one. The move hadn’t just changed my address. It had cut me off from the roles, evidence and recognition that my sense of self was built on.

And then the sentence that changed everything: you are not starting from zero. My problem wasn’t that my experience was worthless. It was that my experience had never been translated — into language, evidence and stories that employers here recognise.

So I stopped applying for two weeks and started building instead. I went back through my Lagos career and listed ten real achievements — the portfolio I cleaned up, the process I fixed, the team I trained — and for each one, I wrote a single line an employer here could actually use. That exercise took 30 minutes on a Tuesday, and it did more for my head than two years of reassurance. For the first time since landing, I could see what I was worth, in writing, in front of me.

Over the following weeks I turned that into a routine. Days 1–5: name the real loss, separate my status from my worth, and build that proof sheet. Days 6–16: translate my CV properly — Nigerian titles mapped to UK, US and Canada equivalents — and map a three-rung bridge: the survival job I had, the bridge role one step up, and the target role I actually deserve. I wrote myself scripts for the questions that used to shrink me: “so what do you do?”, networking messages, and the hardest one — what to tell my family.

My husband noticed before I told him. He said I’d stopped going silent after night shifts and kept talking about “the plan” at dinner. Then my mother. On our weekly video call, I finally told her the truth about my job. I had rehearsed it like an apology. She said, “Ah-ah, is that why you’ve been looking tired? Thank God you told me.” Relief — not disappointment. I cried in the kitchen after that call, and it wasn’t the sad kind.

Let me be honest about what this did and didn’t do. It did not magic a job offer into my inbox, and I won’t pretend otherwise. What it did was give me something I hadn’t had since I landed: a clear, concrete path, a CV that reads like a professional instead of an apology, conversations I could hold my head up in, and the evenings of my life back from the 2am spiral.

When the women in my community started asking me — the delivery driver who used to engineer, the warehouse supervisor who used to run a school — I found myself typing the same steps into WhatsApp again and again. Eventually I sat down and turned my notes into one document, in plain language, for people with our exact background.

I’m not a guru and I’m not a coach. I’m a night-shift care assistant in Leicester who found a method that worked on one very stubborn case: mine. What follows is that method, written down so you don’t have to spend two years finding it.
